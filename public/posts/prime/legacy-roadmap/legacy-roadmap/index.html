Series: The Legacy Roadmap - A conversation between academia and industry on legacy system modernization

May 11, 2026 6-minute read
Renato Teixeira
Modernization
LegacyModernization • SoftwareArchitecture • TechnologyDecisionMaking • EvidenceBasedSoftwareEngineering • SoftwareEngineering

On April 29, 2026, my master’s dissertation was approved by the academic committee. The work is titled “Legacy Roadmap: A Sociotechnical Framework for Decision-Making in Legacy System Modernization in Latin America.” Now, I want to do something I consider as important as completing the research itself: turning part of this knowledge into a more accessible conversation for those who live technology in practice.

Starting on 05/15/2026, I will publish a weekly series of articles based on the dissertation. The idea is not simply to summarize the academic work, because that would be of limited use to those dealing with the industry’s day-to-day reality. The goal is to translate its main findings into more direct language, connecting research, professional practice, architecture, modernization, risk, and decision-making.

In my view, there is one point Software Engineering still needs to address more seriously: the distance between what academia investigates and what industry faces every day. This distance should not be normalized. Academia produces concepts, methods, evidence, and structured ways of understanding complex problems. Industry, on the other hand, deals with real pressure, critical systems, limited budgets, operational risks, delivery urgency, accumulated legacy, and decisions that do not always fit into ideal models.

When these two worlds do not talk to each other, everyone loses. Academia risks producing knowledge that does not reach the places where software is built, maintained, and modernized. Industry risks repeating decisions based only on intuition, urgency, hype, or local experience, without taking advantage of evidence that could make its practices more mature. The purpose of this series is to contribute, even if modestly, to that approximation.

My dissertation starts from a simple premise, but one with deep implications: modernizing legacy systems is not only a technical decision. It is a sociotechnical decision. It naturally involves architecture and technology, but it also involves risk, organizational memory, accumulated experience, operational dependency, cognitive factors, tacit knowledge, culture, and strategy. The research showed that the decision to modernize legacy systems is rarely motivated only by technological factors. It is strongly influenced by organizational elements, risk perception, and the accumulated experience of the professionals involved.

That is what this series will address: modernization not as a simple technology replacement, but as a decision-making process; legacy not as “old software,” but as dependency, heritage, risk, and continuity; transformation not as a generic promise, but as a difficult choice between what to preserve, what to evolve, when to act, and how to reduce uncertainty. The intention is to discuss modernization without relying on silver bullets, but also without turning the topic into an overly academic conversation disconnected from practice.

What will be published in the series Link to heading

Part 1 — Legacy Is Not Age. It Is Dependency. Link to heading

The first article will discuss why defining legacy only by the age of the technology is a trap. A system may use old technology and still be essential. Another may use recent technology and already be difficult to evolve. The central question will be: when does a system stop being merely old and start representing a structural dependency for the organization?

Part 2 — Legacy Systems Survive Because They Still Matter Link to heading

The second article starts from an important provocation: many legacy systems do not remain because “no one had the courage to replace them.” They remain because they work, sustain critical operations, carry business rules, and preserve accumulated knowledge. Before treating legacy as an obstacle, it may be necessary to recognize the value it still delivers.

Part 3 — The Risk of Changing Is Visible. The Risk of Not Changing Is Not Always Visible. Link to heading

Every modernization involves risk, but the industry tends to discuss the risk of change much more than the risk of remaining as is. This article will explore the idea that not modernizing is also a risk choice, even when the organization prefers to treat that posture as prudence, stability, or simple operational continuity.

Part 4 — When Software Becomes Part of the Organization Link to heading

This article will address the concept of system-organization entanglement: situations in which the legacy system no longer merely supports processes, but starts shaping the way the company operates. In these cases, modernizing is not just changing code. It is changing processes, routines, decisions, tacit knowledge, and, in some cases, the organization’s own operational identity.

Part 5 — Postponed Modernization Is Also a Decision Link to heading

Not every organization clearly decides whether to maintain or modernize. Many simply postpone. They create patches, additional layers, exceptions, temporary integrations, and reasonable short-term justifications. The problem is that postponement also accumulates cost, complexity, and risk. This article will discuss postponed modernization as a silent form of decision.

Part 6 — When the System Only Works Because Someone Still Remembers Link to heading

One of the most critical risks of legacy systems is not only in the code, but in the knowledge that never became organizational heritage. Business rules, exceptions, dependencies, and historical decisions often live in the memory of a few people. When those people leave, the system can become an operational black box. This article will be about tacit knowledge, technical memory, and software archaeology.

Part 7 — Before Automating Change, Automate Understanding Link to heading

Automation and AI have an important role in modernization, but there is an evident risk: accelerating changes in systems that have not yet been understood. This article will defend a central idea of the series: in legacy systems, the most valuable automation does not start with transformation, but with the recovery of understanding. Before automating change, we need to automate, or at least expand, comprehension.

Part 8 — Modernizing Is Not Replacing Technology. It Is Governing Continuity. Link to heading

The final article closes the series by presenting the logic of the Legacy Roadmap: a model for organizing modernization decisions based on diagnosis, risk explicitation, cognitive reflection, decision guidelines, and situational technical execution. The final thesis will be direct: technology matters, but it should not come before understanding the context, the risk, and the consequences of the decision.

Why publish this series Link to heading

My intention is to contribute to a more mature conversation about legacy system modernization. A conversation less driven by fads and more guided by evidence; less centered only on tools and more attentive to the decisions that precede any tool; less concerned with repeating generic transformation narratives and more interested in understanding why so many organizations remain tied to systems they know they need to evolve.

Legacy system modernization is one of the most important topics in contemporary Software Engineering, not because it is new, but precisely because it remains persistent. Perhaps part of this persistence comes from the fact that we still treat as a purely technical problem something that, in practice, is also organizational, cognitive, strategic, and human.

Starting on 05/15/2026, I will begin publishing the series “The Legacy Roadmap — legacy system modernization as decision, risk, and sociotechnical evolution.” The purpose is to bring research a little closer to practice and, perhaps, help professionals and organizations make better decisions about the systems that sustain the present — and that may limit or enable the future.
